A single unbroken 10-second drone take. One camera, one continuous move, filmed in one go. There is no edit, no cut, no montage, no shot change and no transition anywhere in it: the picture runs without interruption from the first frame to the last. This is one shot, not a sequence of shots.

THE MOVE
A camera flies low and forward over a frozen fjord at dawn, and it never stops moving forward. It opens gliding past a glowing white circular podium floating on the mirror-calm water with a giant glossy white molar tooth with four roots hovering just above it; the podium and the tooth drift past on the left and out of the picture after about two seconds and never come back. In the same unbroken move the camera keeps flying and leans gently into a wide right-hand curve, the horizon tilting about eighteen degrees, easing off its speed as the curve brings it alongside a dark wet granite cliff standing on the right — almost vertical, cut by deep fissures, with thin ledges of snow, its base plunging straight into the water. For about two seconds it glides past that cliff within a few metres of the rock, at its slowest, so the wet granite fills the whole right half of the picture and its cracks are readable. Still without interruption it eases out of that lean, rolls the other way into an equally wide left-hand curve and gathers speed again through it — and while the granite is still sliding out of the right edge, a smooth glacier-carved white snow dome is already sliding into the left edge, so there is never a moment with no land in the picture. The camera eases off again and for about two seconds glides past that snow dome within a few metres, its rounded white slope and pale blue waterline ice filling the whole left half of the picture. Then, still in the same unbroken move, it levels out, gathers a little speed and flies straight into a low bank of thick fog, which closes over the lens completely, and the take ends inside flat featureless pale blue-white haze with nothing recognisable left in it — no rock, no water, no horizon, no silhouette.

HOW THE CAMERA MOVES — this is the most important part of the shot
The camera only ever travels FORWARD, through the scene. It must never swing, whip, sweep or snap sideways, and it must never rotate on the spot.
Every change of direction is made by FLYING along a curve, never by turning the camera: the heading changes only as fast as the aircraft is travelling, so the view always opens up gradually ahead instead of the scene sliding across the frame.
At every instant, both halves of the picture must move at the same rate. The left side and the right side of the frame travel at the same speed — never one side sweeping past while the other stands still. If the edge of the frame is moving several times faster than the middle, the move is wrong.
Speed changes are gradual and continuous, on a smooth ease-in ease-out curve: fastest in the middle of each curve, slowest while passing each headland. Over any one second the forward speed never more than doubles and never more than halves, and it never changes in a single step.
At its slowest the camera is still drifting forward. It never stops, never freezes, never hovers in place and never flies backwards — and that holds for every instant of the shot, including inside the closing fog. The last second is not a frozen image: the camera is still flying and the fog still drifts past the lens.

WHAT NEVER CHANGES
The mirror water fills the bottom of the picture in every frame until the fog. The dawn light comes from the upper left the whole time and never changes colour, angle or brightness. The palette is cool white and pale blue only. The two headlands keep exactly the same shape, size and side for the whole shot. The water stays glassy and calm. There is only ever one camera, and it is never repositioned, re-framed or teleported — if a moment feels like it wants a cut, it keeps flying instead.

FRAMING RULE
While the camera passes the cliff on the right, the left half of the picture stays completely open — flat mirror water, drifting mist, empty sky, no land and no object at all. While it passes the snow dome on the left, the right half stays completely open in the same way.

TECHNICAL
Photorealistic 3D render, cinematic product-commercial look, 35mm lens, 24 fps, 16:9. 3840x2160 if available, otherwise 1920x1080 — never lower. Every single frame must be sharp enough to stand alone as a still: almost no motion blur, no shutter smear, no grain, no vignette, no lens flare, no depth-of-field blur, no chromatic aberration. Constant exposure and white balance from the first frame to the last. No zoom, no focal-length change, no handheld shake.

AVOID: the camera swinging sideways faster than it flies forward; one side of the frame sweeping past while the other side barely moves; whip pans, snap pans, sudden yaw, the camera rotating on the spot, a lurch or a jolt in the middle of a turn, speed ramps, any single-frame jump in speed or direction; the camera stopping, freezing, hovering or flying backwards at any point, a still or frozen final second; cuts, jump cuts, cross-fades, dissolves, a second shot, an establishing shot followed by another shot, montage, b-roll, the camera teleporting or re-framing; text, letters, numbers, subtitles, watermarks, logos, people, animals, boats, birds, aircraft, buildings; the tooth or podium returning after two seconds; camera shake, orbiting, spinning, barrel rolls; morphing or warping terrain, headlands that change shape, size or swap sides; land or objects on the half of the picture that must stay open; an empty picture with no headland between the two passes; cartoon style, oversaturated colour, warm, golden or orange tint, night; any landscape visible through the closing fog, thin fog at the end, the fog clearing again.
