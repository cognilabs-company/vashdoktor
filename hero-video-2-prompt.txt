A single unbroken 10-second drone take. One camera, one continuous move, filmed in one go. There is no edit, no cut, no montage, no shot change and no transition anywhere in it: the picture runs without interruption from the first frame to the last. This is one shot, not a sequence of shots.

THE MOVE
The take opens inside thick fog — the first frame is flat, even, completely featureless pale blue-white haze filling the whole picture. A camera is already flying low and forward through it and never stops moving forward. Over about a second the fog thins evenly and the frozen fjord at dawn comes out of it: mirror-calm water below, low mist drifting, and a sharp black rock spire standing ahead on the right. In the same unbroken move the camera banks gently to the right like an aircraft, the horizon tilting about eighteen degrees, slowing down as it curves toward that spire — jagged and serrated, almost bare stone dusted with frost, thin streaks of snow caught in its gullies. For about two seconds it glides past the spire within a few metres of the rock, at its slowest, so the black stone fills the whole right half of the picture and its edges are readable. Still without interruption it rolls out of that bank, rolls the other way into a wide left-hand turn and speeds up through the turn — and while the spire is still sliding out of the right edge, a broad snow ridge with a long gentle shoulder and dark layered rock bands is already sliding into the left edge, so there is never a moment with no land in the picture. The camera slows again and for about two seconds glides past that ridge within a few metres, its wide white slope filling the whole left half of the picture. Then, still in the same unbroken move, it rolls level and pitches up in one smooth arc, climbing steeply between the last peaks and accelerating as it rises, until every summit has sunk below the bottom edge and left the picture entirely; it keeps climbing through the high cloud layer into thick fog, which closes over the lens completely, and the take ends held on flat featureless pale blue-white haze with nothing recognisable left in it — no mountain, no water, no horizon, no silhouette.

WHAT NEVER CHANGES
The mirror water fills the bottom of the picture in every frame between the opening fog and the climb. The dawn light comes from the upper left the whole time and never changes colour, angle or brightness. The palette is cool white and pale blue only. The two headlands keep exactly the same shape, size and side. The water stays glassy and calm. There is only ever one camera, and it is never repositioned, re-framed or teleported — if a moment feels like it wants a cut, it keeps flying instead.

FRAMING RULE
While the camera passes the spire on the right, the left half of the picture stays completely open — flat mirror water, drifting mist, empty sky, no land and no object at all. While it passes the snow ridge on the left, the right half stays completely open in the same way.

HOW THE CAMERA MOVES — this is the most important part of the shot
The camera only ever travels FORWARD, through the scene. It must never swing, whip, sweep or snap sideways, and it must never rotate on the spot.
Every change of direction is made by FLYING along a curve, never by turning the camera: the heading changes only as fast as the aircraft is travelling, so the view always opens up gradually ahead instead of the scene sliding across the frame.
At every instant, both halves of the picture must move at the same rate. The left side and the right side of the frame travel at the same speed — never one side sweeping past while the other stands still. If the edge of the frame is moving several times faster than the middle, the move is wrong.
Speed changes are gradual and continuous, on a smooth ease-in ease-out curve: fastest in the middle of each curve, slowest while passing each headland, rising again into the final climb. Over any one second the forward speed never more than doubles and never more than halves, and it never changes in a single step.
At its slowest the camera is still drifting forward. It never stops, never freezes, never hovers in place and never flies backwards — and that holds for every instant of the shot, including inside the opening and the closing fog. The first and last seconds are not frozen images: the camera is still flying and the fog still drifts past the lens.

TECHNICAL
Photorealistic 3D render, cinematic product-commercial look, 35mm lens, 16:9, 24 fps, 3840x2160 if available, otherwise 1920x1080 — never lower. Every single frame must be sharp enough to stand alone as a still: almost no motion blur, no shutter smear, no grain, no vignette, no lens flare, no depth-of-field blur, no chromatic aberration. Constant exposure and white balance. No zoom, no focal-length change, no handheld shake.

AVOID: the camera swinging sideways faster than it flies forward; one side of the frame sweeping past while the other side barely moves; whip pans, snap pans, sudden yaw, the camera rotating on the spot, a lurch or a jolt in the middle of a turn, speed ramps, any single-frame jump in speed or direction; the camera stopping, freezing, hovering or flying backwards at any point, a still or frozen first or final second; cuts, jump cuts, cross-fades, dissolves, a second shot, an establishing shot followed by another shot, montage, b-roll, the camera teleporting or re-framing; text, letters, numbers, subtitles, watermarks, logos, people, animals, boats, birds, aircraft, buildings; a tooth, a podium or any object floating on the water; camera shake, whip pans, orbiting, spinning, barrel rolls, sudden yaw; the camera stopping, freezing or flying backwards; morphing or warping terrain, headlands that change shape or swap sides; land or objects on the half of the picture that must stay open; an empty picture with no headland between the two passes; the camera staying level at the end instead of climbing, peaks still visible beside or above the camera in the last two seconds, the climb flattening out before the fog closes; cartoon style, oversaturated colour, warm, golden or orange tint, night; any landscape visible through the opening or closing fog, thin fog at the start or the end, the fog clearing again.
